Sumida STEAM Fes 2025

On January 11, 2026, iGEM Keio participated in Sumida STEAM Fes 2025, an educational event for elementary, junior high, and high school students. Since many visitors were already interested in robotics, programming, and engineering, we wanted to broaden their view of STEAM by introducing biology and synthetic biology as another field of future technology.

What We Did

We prepared three activities: a poster exhibition, the Synthetic Biology Tree, and an original browser game called Bio Bricks Catch Game. The poster introduced examples of useful microbial functions, while the game helped children become interested in genetic circuits by collecting biological parts in the order of promoter, RBS, GFP, and terminator.

In the Synthetic Biology Tree activity, we asked visitors questions such as “What would you like to do with synthetic biology?” and “What kind of microorganism would you like to create?” Participants wrote their ideas on paper leaves and added them to the tree.

Impact

We collected 56 ideas from visitors. Their ideas ranged from practical applications, such as microorganisms that produce energy, to imaginative ideas, such as microorganisms that help with studying. The game was especially popular among elementary school students and served as an effective icebreaker.

These activities also created opportunities for deeper conversations with parents, technical college students, university students, and educators about iGEM, synthetic biology, and possible applications of biotechnology.

What We Learned

We learned that children can engage creatively with synthetic biology when they are given accessible tools and open-ended questions. At the same time, some visitors raised questions about ethics, applications, and biosafety, including concerns about whether E. coli could be harmful. This reminded us that education should not only communicate excitement, but also include careful discussion of safety and responsibility.

iGEM Japan Meetup 2026 1st

On March 25, 2026, iGEM Keio participated in the iGEM Japan Meetup 2026 1st, held at the Toyama Office of the National Institute of Infectious Diseases. The event brought together Japanese iGEM teams, instructors, iGEM Alumni, and other members of the iGEM community in a hybrid format.

What We Did

We presented our early-stage project idea through both a short talk and a poster session. In the short talk, we introduced our project background, the problem we wanted to address, and our current design concept. In the poster session, we discussed our idea in more detail with students, instructors, and iGEM Alumni.

Impact

The meetup helped us build connections with other domestic iGEM teams and receive feedback from multiple perspectives. We received comments on technical feasibility, ethical considerations, possible social implementation, and how our project differs from existing research.

Many questions focused on the concreteness of our design, the roadmap toward implementation, and the social impact of our project. These comments helped us identify which parts of our idea were clear and which parts still needed improvement.

What We Learned

We learned that an iGEM project must be communicated not only as a scientific idea, but also as a technically feasible, ethically responsible, and socially meaningful proposal. Because this meetup took place early in the season, the feedback we received was especially valuable for refining our project direction.

We also realized the importance of preparing expected questions in advance and recording discussion points more systematically so that feedback can be used more effectively after the event.

Earth Day Tokyo 2026

On April 18–19, 2026, iGEM Keio participated in Earth Day Tokyo 2026, held at Yoyogi Park. Since Earth Day Tokyo is a large public event focused on environmental action, we used this opportunity to introduce how synthetic biology could contribute to solving environmental problems.

What We Did

We created a booth exhibition with posters and dialogue-based communication. Starting from familiar environmental issues such as plastic waste, increasing CO₂ levels, and food-related challenges, we introduced examples of microbial functions, including PETase-based plastic degradation, CO₂ fixation by cyanobacteria, and nitrogen fixation by rhizobia.

To make the content accessible, we used illustrations and reduced technical terms as much as possible. We framed the booth around a simple question: “Can microorganisms help save the Earth?”

Impact

We talked with visitors from a wide range of generations, from small children to elderly people. Many visitors showed interest in the idea that microorganisms could be used as tools for environmental problem-solving. Some also became interested in iGEM and our research activities.

At the same time, visitors asked important questions about the use of E. coli, biosafety, outdoor applications, and possible effects on ecosystems. These conversations showed that public interest in biotechnology is closely connected to questions of safety, ethics, and social acceptance.

What We Learned

We learned that environmental issues are an effective entry point for communicating synthetic biology to the general public. However, we also realized that public education cannot focus only on the benefits of technology. It must also explain risks, limitations, and safety measures in a way that is understandable to non-specialists.

For future outreach activities, we would like to prepare materials at different levels of difficulty, record quantitative data such as visitor numbers, and strengthen our answers to expected questions about ethics and biosafety.
