Interview with Associate Professor Kenji Tajima

As one possible application of our technology for linearly connecting E. coli cells, we explored the creation of structurally controlled bacterial cellulose through cell-orientation control. To deepen our understanding, we interviewed Associate Professor Kenji Tajima from Hokkaido University, an expert in bacterial cellulose production.

Through this interview, we discussed the differences between cellulose production in acetic acid bacteria and E. coli, the possibility of controlling cellulose fiber orientation, metabolic engineering, and potential social applications.

/static/images/hupra/tajima.png

What We Learned

Differences in Cellulose Formation Mechanisms

Professor Tajima explained that acetic acid bacteria, known for producing nata de coco, have cellulose synthase complexes aligned almost linearly on the cell membrane. Because cellulose is secreted from a localized region, the molecular chains assemble in an ordered manner, producing highly crystalline and mechanically strong fibers.

In contrast, in E. coli, cellulose synthase complexes tend to localize near the cell poles, but cellulose secretion is not organized in the same linear manner. This suggested to us that simply introducing cellulose-related genes may not be sufficient to reproduce the highly ordered structure found in acetic acid bacteria.

Importance of Metabolic Engineering

We also learned that cellulose production requires sufficient supply of UDP-glucose, a precursor of cellulose. While acetic acid bacteria naturally have a well-developed pathway for cellulose production, E. coli may require metabolic engineering to strengthen UDP-glucose supply.

Professor Tajima also shared that the cellulose conversion efficiency of acetic acid bacteria is about 15%, and that metabolic engineering may improve this value to around 30%. This helped us understand that structural control must be considered together with metabolic productivity.

Orientation Control and Material Strength

We discussed the relationship between fiber orientation and material strength. In static culture, bacterial cellulose generally forms a three-dimensional network. However, when stretching or flow is applied, fibers can become aligned in one direction, which can greatly improve mechanical strength.

Highly crystalline cellulose has been reported to reach tensile strengths of about 3 GPa, comparable to carbon fiber. This indicated that orientation control may be an important strategy for creating stronger bacterial cellulose materials.

Cell Movement and Structural Control

Professor Tajima pointed out that bacteria move while secreting cellulose, which means that simply fixing the cells may interfere with fiber formation. He introduced an approach based on studies by Professor Tetsuo Kondo at Kyushu University, in which oriented sheets are used to guide secreted cellulose along rail-like structures.

This idea was important for our project because it suggested that structure could be controlled without completely immobilizing the cells.

How This Shaped Our Project

This interview helped us rethink our project not only as a method for connecting cells, but also as a possible platform for controlling the structure of biomaterials. If E. coli cells can be connected in a defined orientation, they may guide cellulose secretion in a more ordered direction and contribute to the creation of structurally controlled bacterial cellulose.

We also discussed the possibility of controlling fiber thickness. In acetic acid bacteria, the fiber diameter generated by synthase complex arrays is roughly 50–170 nm. This suggested that controlling the number and arrangement of connected cells could potentially allow us to design fiber diameter and material architecture.

Another concern was whether cell connections would be broken during shaking or rotating culture. Professor Tajima suggested that the antibody-antigen binding system used in our project may help maintain connections between E. coli cells, making cellulose production by connected cells a possible direction to explore.

Possible Applications

From this discussion, we identified several promising applications of structurally controlled bacterial cellulose, including wound dressings, bacterial cellulose straws, and biofilters. Other possible applications include periodontal treatment sheets, separation membranes, catalyst supports, electrochromic devices, and reinforcing fibers.

We also learned that some medical applications of bacterial cellulose have already been implemented in Europe. This showed us that bacterial cellulose is not only an interesting research target, but also a material with realistic social implementation potential.

Reflection

Through this interview, we learned that the value of our project depends not only on whether we can connect cells, but also on whether that connection can lead to meaningful control of biological structure. The discussion with Professor Tajima helped us connect our cell-alignment technology to a concrete material application and clarified the technical challenges we need to consider, such as secretion direction, metabolic supply, cell movement, and culture conditions.

This interview therefore expanded our understanding of how our project could contribute to synthetic biology: by using engineered cell-cell connections as a tool for designing not only cellular behavior, but also higher-order biomaterial structures.

Interview with Professor Isogai

To further explore possible applications of orientation-controlled bacterial cellulose, we interviewed Professor Isogai from the University of Tokyo. Bacterial cellulose is a bio-based material known for its high safety, mechanical strength, and purity. However, its social implementation still faces challenges, including how to differentiate it from plant-derived cellulose and how to improve production efficiency.

In this interview, we discussed cellulose biosynthesis using E. coli, the historical and social background of cellulose materials, possible applications of bacterial cellulose, and the originality of our approach: connecting bacterial cells in a linear arrangement.

/static/images/hupra/isogai.png

What We Learned

Cellulose Formation Requires More Than Gene Introduction

Professor Isogai explained that cellulose production is not simply achieved by introducing cellulose-related enzymes. In natural bacterial cellulose production, cells move while secreting cellulose fibers, and this movement contributes to the formation of higher-order structures.

He also pointed out that protein complexes called “rosettes” and fine pores for cellulose secretion are essential for proper fiber formation. If cellulose synthesis is attempted in organisms such as E. coli, cellulose may accumulate inside the cell or form a disordered structure similar to cellulose II. This made us realize that experimental validation of the resulting cellulose structure is essential.

Historical Lessons from Cellulose Materials

We also learned that cellulose has a long social and industrial history. In the past, plant-derived cellulose supported Japanese industry through materials such as rayon. However, domestic production declined partly due to environmental problems in the manufacturing process.

There were also past trends such as “cellulose without cutting trees” and fuel production using highly active cellulases. However, these approaches faced limitations, especially because of low conversion efficiency. Professor Isogai explained that only about 20% of the energy can be converted into cellulose, which has been a major barrier to industrial implementation.

This history showed us that bacterial cellulose cannot be socially implemented only by emphasizing that it is bio-based. It must provide value that plant-derived cellulose or petroleum-based polymers cannot easily offer.

Where Bacterial Cellulose Can Be Valuable

Professor Isogai emphasized that the strengths of bacterial cellulose are its flexibility, biocompatibility, and high purity. These features are especially important for applications where safety and reliability are required.

He suggested that promising fields for bacterial cellulose include energy, electronics, and medical applications. In particular, medical devices may benefit from the high purity and biological safety of bacterial cellulose.

For example, he mentioned that creating hollow structures such as artificial blood vessels using bacterial cellulose would be extremely challenging, but also highly valuable. This helped us understand that high-value applications may be more realistic than simply replacing ordinary industrial cellulose materials.

How This Shaped Our Project

Professor Isogai found our idea of linearly connecting E. coli cells very interesting. He suggested that, as a first milestone, achieving a connection of around 10 cells would be a meaningful goal. This gave us a more concrete target for evaluating whether our cell-connection system can actually control bacterial arrangement.

The interview also changed how we think about possible outcomes. Even if cellulose production in connected E. coli does not proceed exactly as expected, the resulting structures may still provide valuable scientific insights. For example, unexpected cellulose accumulation, disordered fiber formation, or unusual morphology could reveal new information about how cellular arrangement affects biomaterial formation.

Through this discussion, we realized that our project should not be framed only as a method to improve cellulose production efficiency. Instead, it can be positioned as an attempt to explore whether engineered cell-cell connections can create new forms of biological structure control.

Reflection

This interview helped us understand both the potential and the difficulty of applying our technology to bacterial cellulose. Bacterial cellulose has attractive properties, but its social implementation requires clear advantages over existing materials and careful consideration of production efficiency, structure, and application fields.

We also learned that the originality of our project lies in controlling the spatial arrangement of cells themselves. By connecting E. coli cells in a defined orientation, we may be able to investigate how cell alignment affects cellulose formation and whether this can lead to new material structures.

Professor Isogai’s feedback gave us a clearer direction: first demonstrate controlled linear cell connection, then examine how this arrangement influences cellulose production and structure. This helped us refine our project from a broad application idea into a more concrete research question.

Discussion with Dr. Kazufumi Hosoda on Linear E. coli Co-culture Systems

We discussed the feasibility, significance, and future challenges of using linearly aligned E. coli cells for co-culture systems with Dr. Kazufumi Hosoda, Senior Researcher at the Center for Information and Neural Networks, National Institute of Information and Communications Technology (NICT).

The discussion focused on the difficulty of creating linear cell arrangements, the challenges of maintaining alternating two-strain patterns, and the type of applications that could justify such a technically challenging approach.

/static/images/hupra/hosoda.png

What We Learned

Linear Cell Connection Is a Challenging Goal

Dr. Hosoda pointed out that creating linearly connected bacteria is a highly challenging goal. In particular, achieving binding only at the cell poles is technically difficult. However, he also suggested that even partial localization of binding molecules to the cell ends would be a meaningful first step.

This feedback helped us understand that our project should not define success only as perfect control from the beginning. Instead, we should evaluate partial progress, such as biased localization or the formation of short linear chains, as important milestones.

Challenges in Maintaining Alternating Co-culture Patterns

We also discussed the possibility of creating alternating two-strain arrangements such as “ABAB...” structures. A major concern was what happens after cell division. Even if A and B cells are initially arranged alternately, division may produce patterns such as “AABB,” causing the same type of cells to cluster together.

Dr. Hosoda advised us that before focusing on experimental feasibility, we should first draw an ideal model of the process and predict what happens at each stage. Without such a model, it would be difficult to determine why an experiment failed or which step needs to be improved. This made us realize the importance of clarifying the dynamic process we expect to occur.

Possibility of Stopping Cell Division

Another possible strategy discussed was to stop cell growth while allowing metabolic reactions to continue. For example, removing an essential nutrient could suppress cell division after the cells are connected, allowing the arranged structure to be maintained for a longer time.

However, this idea also raised an important question: if the cells are no longer dividing, why is a linear arrangement necessary? Would simple mixing or centrifugation be enough to bring cells into contact? This question helped us recognize that we need to explain the specific advantage of linear alignment, not only the fact that cells are close to one another.

Complexity of Two-strain Co-culture

We also learned that two-strain co-culture systems are inherently complex. Different strains may have different growth rates and nutrient consumption patterns, so even a small difference can cause one strain to dominate the system. In artificial co-culture systems, cells may also experience stress and undergo adaptive evolution, reducing reproducibility.

Cell density is another important factor. If the culture is too dilute, cooperative interactions may not occur. If it is too dense, interactions may become excessive or difficult to control. These points showed us that co-culture systems are much more difficult to predict than monocultures.

How This Shaped Our Project

Although many technical challenges were raised, the discussion also confirmed that spatial proximity between cells can be valuable. In systems where two cell types depend on each other, bringing them close together may promote cooperative behavior. We also discussed examples in which the spatial arrangement of two strains on an agar plate affects their growth patterns.

This helped us understand that the value of our project is not simply in connecting cells, but in controlling cell-cell distance and spatial relationships. Linear arrangement could become a tool for studying or designing interactions between different cell types.

At the same time, Dr. Hosoda pointed out that co-culture alone may not be a strong enough application. To communicate the importance of our project, we need a larger and more impactful vision. Possible directions include drug delivery using linearly connected bacteria, cancer therapy, environmental applications, and biological systems with computational functions.

This feedback helped us refine our message: our project is difficult, but it is worth pursuing because controlled bacterial alignment could open new ways to design spatial biological systems.

Reflection

Overall, this discussion made us recognize both the difficulty and the potential impact of our project. Creating linearly connected bacteria is a challenging goal, especially when considering polar localization, cell division, and long-term structural maintenance.

However, if successful, the technology could provide a new platform for controlling the spatial organization of living cells. Based on this feedback, we decided that our first goal should be to demonstrate linear cell connection itself. At the same time, we need to develop stronger application scenarios that clearly explain why this difficult challenge is meaningful.

This discussion therefore shifted our thinking from “Can this co-culture system work?” to “What kind of spatial control would make this technology valuable?” It helped us focus on both experimental milestones and the broader impact of engineered cell arrangement.
